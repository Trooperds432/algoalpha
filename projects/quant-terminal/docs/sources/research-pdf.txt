Building a Python-Native Quantitative Trading
Terminal: Architecture, Platform Landscape and
Implementation Blueprint
Executive summary
The strongest design for the terminal you describe is a Python-first, event-driven quantitative
research and trading platform with a vectorised acceleration layer, rather than a MATLAB-first
application. The core should expose one strategy interface that can run against historical data, walk-
forward windows, parameter-search jobs, Monte Carlo paths, paper trading and—later—the same live
broker adapters. This is broadly the architectural lesson visible across LEAN/QuantConnect, Backtrader ,
Zipline and QuantRocket: separate the strategy from data, brokerage/execution simulation, portfolio
accounting and reporting. LEAN, in particular , explicitly separates fill, slippage, fee, buying-power and
brokerage models instead of embedding these assumptions inside strategy code. 
Recommendation: build in Python, with MATLAB supported only as an optional research/model
integration layer . MATLAB is an excellent numerical research environment and can call Python directly or
out-of-process, but making it the centre of a terminal whose defining feature is “run arbitrary Python
algorithms” adds licensing, deployment and language-boundary complexity without a compensating
advantage. MathWorks itself supports direct Python calls, pyrun , pyrunfile , DataFrame/table
interchange and out-of-process Python execution, which makes MATLAB better suited here as an
optional client rather than the execution kernel. 
The recommended system is not purely vectorised and not purely event-driven:
Use an event-driven engine for execution-sensitive backtests, order state, partial fills, market/
limit/stop orders, margin, paper trading and eventual live operation.
Use a vectorised engine or vectorised analysis path for signal research and thousands of
parameter combinations. Vectorbt demonstrates the huge computational advantage of array/
Numba-style testing, while Backtrader , Zipline and LEAN illustrate the richer semantics
obtainable from an event engine. 
Keep optimisation, walk-forward validation and Monte Carlo orchestration outside the
individual strategy, so any strategy can be subjected to the same research protocol.
Record every run as an immutable experiment containing configuration, code/data version,
random seed, fills, trades, positions, equity and analytics. This makes a result reproducible rather
than merely visually attractive.
Start with daily/minute equities and ETFs, market/limit/stop orders, realistic costs, multi-asset
portfolios, parameter optimisation, walk-forward testing, Monte Carlo and comprehensive
analytics. Do not begin with HFT, tick-level limit-order-book simulation, options margin or twenty
broker integrations.
A credible MVP is approximately 12–16 full-time engineer-weeks for one strong Python developer,
assuming bar-based equities/ETFs and no production live trading. A robust paper/live release is more
reasonably 20–30 engineer-weeks because reconciliation, connectivity failures, broker semantics,

The commercial comparison also suggests there is a genuine niche for what you are proposing.
MultiCharts currently has native strategy backtesting, genetic optimisation, walk-forward analysis,
Portfolio Trader and automated trading, while its Python support is still advertised as a beta. AmiBroker
has unusually mature native optimisation, walk-forward and Monte Carlo analysis, but uses AFL rather
than Python. QuantConnect/LEAN has arguably the strongest open execution/modelling foundation
and Python support, but its hosted optimisation workflow is currently centred on grid search and leaves
richer walk-forward/Monte Carlo research protocols to the user . QuantRocket is probably the closest
existing Python-centric architectural analogue: it supports multiple Python backtesters, live trading,
parameter scans and explicit rolling/expanding walk-forward machine-learning workflows. 
The resulting product should feel like this:
MultiCharts/AmiBroker research workflow + Python ecosystem + LEAN-style market
modelling + vectorbt-style high-throughput research + pyfolio-style tear sheets.
That combination is more compelling than cloning any single existing platform.

Two conclusions follow from this comparison.
First, AmiBroker is the benchmark for the research workflow you are trying to reproduce: optimise,
walk forward, Monte Carlo, inspect results. Its walk-forward facility supports in-sample/out-of-sample
windows and combines OOS results, while its Monte Carlo analyser explicitly resamples trade lists or
portfolio-equity changes and can generate distributions of outcomes. 
Second, LEAN is the better benchmark for market mechanics. Its “reality modelling” deliberately
decomposes execution assumptions into fill, slippage, fee, brokerage, buying power , settlement, short
availability, margin-interest and other models. That decomposition is precisely how your own engine
should be structured. 
Your terminal can therefore differentiate by combining those two philosophies while making Python
the strategy language.

Architecture and technology decision
The central design principle should be:
A strategy expresses intent; the engine owns market mechanics.
A strategy should say:
context.order_target_percent("SPY", 0.50)
not:
# strategy manually modifies cash, calculates commission,
# assumes it filled itself and updates positions
Orders flow through a broker/execution model, which generates fills, which change the portfolio. This
mirrors the event-driven separation in mature frameworks such as LEAN, Zipline and Backtrader .

LEAN's Engine/AlgorithmManager.cs  is particularly worth reading. The repository places the 
AlgorithmManager  inside the engine and passes the live/backtest mode into it, while the engine
launcher composes handlers around the manager; that is a useful reference for keeping strategy
execution separate from the rest of the terminal. 
Then study LEAN's documented “reality models” in parallel with the source. Its architecture recognises
Fill, Slippage, Fee, Brokerage, Buying Power , Settlement, short-availability and other security-specific
models rather than treating execution as one monolithic function. 
Backtrader's Cerebro  is useful for almost the opposite reason: it is conceptually compact. Official
documentation describes Cerebro as the central component that collects data feeds, strategies,
observers, analyzers and writers, then executes either backtesting or live workflows.  That makes it
an excellent educational reference for your first Python engine.
Zipline is worth studying for API ergonomics. Its core is explicitly event-driven, integrates results
through pandas and was designed so scientific libraries such as SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-learn and
Matplotlib could be used around an algorithm.  Current zipline-reloaded  keeps that model
alive, while QuantRocket demonstrates how Zipline can be embedded inside a broader trading terminal
and extended to live operation. 
For your reporting subsystem, pyfolio may be the most immediately reusable intellectual reference. The
maintained pyfolio-reloaded  project describes its central concept as a set of tear sheets
combining plots and summary statistics.  The original modules remain especially instructive:
pyfolio/timeseries.py  contains risk/time-series statistics and turnover integration. 
pyfolio/tears.py  coordinates the report/tear-sheet layers rather than cramming all calculations
into plotting functions. 
pyfolio/plotting.py  provides the chart layer separately. 
Empyrical-reloaded is then worth studying as the lower-level metrics package; it explicitly describes
itself as common financial risk/performance metrics used by Zipline and pyfolio. 
Vectorbt should influence the optimisation execution path, not necessarily your brokerage
architecture. Its fundamental trick is to represent many parameter combinations as arrays and
accelerate portfolio simulation instead of launching a full Python event loop for every strategy/
parameter combination.  A sophisticated terminal could therefore expose two engines:

The risk engine should sit between strategy intent and execution:
Controls worth adding before serious live trading include maximum order notional, maximum position
concentration, gross/net exposure limits, maximum leverage, price collars, stale-market-data
protection, maximum daily loss, maximum drawdown, message-rate limits, duplicate-order protection
and a manual kill switch. These are not merely nice engineering features: the FCA's algorithmic-trading
requirements for regulated firms include resilient systems, capacity, trading thresholds/limits,
prevention of erroneous orders, testing and monitoring. 
The FCA's August 2025 review is particularly relevant to your architecture. It highlights algorithm
inventories, formal deployment/change processes, simulation testing using stressed scenarios, robust
pre- and post-trade controls and market-abuse surveillance as important practices for regulated
algorithmic trading firms.  That strongly supports building versioning, simulation/stress tests and
risk controls into the terminal from the beginning rather than bolting them on after connecting a
broker .
For the US institutional/broker context, SEC Rule 15c3-5 requires broker-dealers with market access to
operate financial and regulatory risk-management controls.  FINRA likewise examines algorithm
approval, performance testing and supervision in algorithmic/HFT contexts.  These rules do not
mean a private research application automatically becomes regulated; the regulatory perimeter
depends on who is trading, whose assets are involved, where orders are routed and what services are
offered. A commercial/client-facing deployment should therefore obtain jurisdiction-specific legal advice
rather than inferring regulatory status from the software architecture alone.
